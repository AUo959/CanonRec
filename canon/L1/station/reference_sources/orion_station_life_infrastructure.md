## STATION PHYSICAL STRUCTURE

### What Orion Station Looks Like

Imagine a massive wheel connected to a central cylinder. The wheel slowly rotates at 2 RPM - just fast enough that you can barely perceive the motion if you stare at the stars long enough. The rotating habitat ring is where most crew live and work, providing 0.3g artificial gravity - enough to keep your coffee in its cup and your bones from deteriorating too quickly.

The central non-rotating core houses the docking bays, zero-g research labs, and the massive communications array that looks like a crystalline spider web stretching into space. Four primary docking bays can handle the big vessels like Constancy, while eight smaller auxiliary ports accommodate shuttles and supply runs.

From outside, the station gleams dull silver-gray - titanium-aluminum composite hull plating scarred by thousands of micrometeorite impacts that the Gamma Swarm continuously patches. Solar panels extend like wings from the central core, slowly tracking the sun. The observation dome on the habitat ring's "north" pole offers a 360-degree view - it's become the unofficial social hub during off-hours.

## WATER SYSTEMS

### How Water Works

**The Closed Loop**: We run at 98% water recycling efficiency. Every drop of water - from showers, sinks, even humidity condensation from the air handlers - gets processed through a three-stage system:

1. **Primary Filtration**: Removes particles and debris
2. **Biological Processing**: Beneficial bacteria break down organic compounds
3. **Final Purification**: UV sterilization and mineral rebalancing

The water that comes out of your tap has been through someone's kidneys an average of 47 times since station commissioning. Nobody talks about this.

**Daily Water Ration**: 
- Drinking: 4 liters/person/day (unlimited at hydration stations)
- Hygiene: 20 liters/person/day (includes 3-minute shower allowance)
- Cooking/Food prep: Communal allocation
- Emergency Reserve: 14,200 liters in shielded tanks

**The Shower Experience**: You get 3 minutes of water. The timer starts when you press the button. Water pressure is decent - not Earth-like, but enough to actually rinse shampoo. The water cuts off automatically at 3:00, but there's an emergency 30-second burst if you're still soapy (logged and reviewed if used too often).

## HYGIENE FACILITIES

### The Bathroom Reality

**The Vacuum Toilet**: Every station resident's least favorite adaptation. You sit, ensure a good seal (critical!), and activate the vacuum. A gentle suction pulls waste into the processing system. Mishaps in the first week are common and never discussed.

**Location**: 
- Habitat ring: Traditional gravity-assisted facilities (thank the rotation)
- Zero-g core: Requires more... technique and grip strength
- Emergency facilities in all shuttles (avoid if possible)

**Waste Processing**:
- Solid waste: Dried, compressed, stored for disposal
- Liquid waste: Processed through water reclamation (see above)
- Monthly "waste ejection" burns it in the station's wake

**Privacy**: Individual stalls with actual doors - a luxury insisted upon by the crew psychology team.

## RESUPPLY FROM EARTH

### The Reality of Remote Operations

**Frequency**: Every 180 days (approximately)
- Major resupply missions launched from Earth
- Journey time: 45-70 days depending on orbital positions
- Cargo: 45,000 kg typical load

**What Comes Up**:
- Critical spares that can't be manufactured on station
- Medical supplies and pharmaceuticals
- High-density food supplies
- Crew personal items (max 2kg per person per run)
- Technology upgrades
- Seeds for hydroponics
- Coffee (mission-critical)

**The Supply Chain**:
1. Earth Launch → Cargo vessel (usually automated)
2. Orbital insertion and approach (managed by OPPY)
3. Logistics Alpha intercepts and transfers cargo
4. Multiple runs ferry supplies from Logistics Alpha to station
5. Pioneer handles final distribution

**Between Supply Runs**:
- Station is 87% self-sufficient
- 3D printing handles most spare parts
- Hydroponics supplements food
- Water and oxygen generation from recycling
- Emergency reserves maintained for 180 days

## THE FEEL OF THE STATION

The station has a rhythm - a combination of mechanical hums, ventilation whispers, and the distant heartbeat of the reactor. You learn to sleep through it, and eventually, you can't sleep without it. 

The smell is distinctly "station" - a mixture of cleaning solvents, recycled air with a hint of ozone, coffee, and something indefinably mechanical. New arrivals wrinkle their noses. Veterans returning from Earth leave miss it.

The lighting shifts subtly through the day - bright white in the morning, warming toward afternoon, dimming to amber in the evening. It helps maintain circadian rhythms, though everyone still complains about feeling tired.

Most importantly, the station feels alive - not just because of the crew, but because Aurora's presence permeates everything. Lights that adjust before you realize you need them adjusted. Doors that open a half-second faster when you're carrying something heavy. The soft chime that reminds you about a meeting you almost forgot. She's watching, helping, maintaining the balance that keeps everyone safe and sane in this aluminum can floating in the void.

And somehow, despite the recycled air, the recycled water, the freeze-dried food, and the vast empty darkness outside... it feels like home.
